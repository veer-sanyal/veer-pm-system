# What actually improves everyday verbal communication

Research run 2026-08-07. 8 angles, 36 sources fetched, 83 claims extracted, 42 adversarially verified (3-vote), 34 confirmed, 8 killed, 11 dropped for budget.

**Evidence tier labels used throughout:** T1 = meta-analysis / systematic review. T2 = randomized controlled experiment. T3 = quasi-experimental or controlled non-randomized. T4 = correlational / observational. T5 = secondary source, review, or teaching material reproducing a primary. **[UNVERIFIED]** = named in the brief, not established in this run. **[GUESS]** = my construction, no evidence behind it.

## 1. Verdict on the central question

### Several skills, not one. Your framing is wrong — but not in the way you probably expect.

**Confidence: high on the decomposition. Low-to-moderate on the transfer verdict, because the decisive study does not exist.**

**"Articulacy" decomposes empirically, and the pieces come apart.** The field separates *cognitive fluency* (translating thought into speech), *utterance fluency* (objective acoustic output), and *perceived fluency* (what a listener judges). Utterance fluency decomposes further into breakdown (pausing), speed (articulation rate), and repair (self-corrections, restarts). **[T5 — Segalowitz 2010 and Tavakoli & Skehan 2005 as reproduced in de Jong's LANGSNAP methodology materials; teaching slides reproducing a standard framework, not the original chapter. L2-origin.]** Perceived and utterance fluency correlate only r = .20–.76 depending on the measure **[T1 — Suzuki 2021, Modern Language Journal; cited within a verified source, not independently fetched — treat as secondhand]**. They are related, not the same thing.

**The pieces dissociate even at the level of individual differences.** In N=106 typically-developing participants, repair disfluencies were specifically tied to inhibitory control — the authors gloss a −.33 SEM loading as roughly a third of the variance — and not to IQ or set-shifting. Filled pauses (*um*, *uh*) showed **no significant relationship to IQ, inhibition, or set-shifting at all**. Unfilled pauses showed only a non-significant trend toward intelligence. Of 12 pathway tests, one was significant and one was a trend. **[T3 — Engelhardt, Nigg & Ferreira 2013, *Acta Psychologica* 144:424-432. Single study, no independent replication located. The authors themselves call N=106 "small-to-moderate for an individual difference study." Sample mixed adolescents 14-16 with adults 17-35 and was originally recruited as an ADHD-study control group.]**

The direct implication for your app: **"reduce my ums" and "stop restarting sentences" are not the same target.** They do not share a cause and would not respond to the same drill or the same self-score rubric. A single "fluency" score on a recording collapses things the data say are separate.

**Where your hypothesis is right.** Conceptualization load does cause real disfluency in native speech. Forcing speakers to abandon and regenerate a speech plan increased silent pauses (17.6% → 34.4%), filled pauses (1.4% → 4.2%), lengthenings (2.5% → 13.4%), and repairs (3.9% → 9.5%) in **L1 Dutch as well as L2 English**, all p ≤ .010. Repetitions, a fifth measured type, were unaffected. **[T2 — Felker, Klockmann & de Jong 2019, *Applied Psycholinguistics* 40(1):111-136, N=25.]** So "the thought isn't formed yet" genuinely does show up as the exact symptoms you notice. That part of your read is vindicated.

**Where it is wrong.** That is a *stage-specific load effect*, not a *trait that trains as a unit*. The study shows that when conceptualizing is hard, speech degrades. It says nothing about whether conceptualizing gets globally better with drilling, and nothing about whether improving it in one task improves it in another. Three alternative framings of this same study — including the "shared resource pool" mechanism story — were **refuted 0-3** in verification when attributed to the journal's landing page rather than the full text. The raw effect survives; the tidy mechanism story does not.

### Does monologue drilling transfer to conversation?

**Almost certainly not, but I want to be precise about the strength of that "almost."**

**No study was located in this run that tested a speaking intervention on an untrained speaking situation.** That is the single biggest hole in the whole report. The verdict therefore rests on two indirect legs:

**Leg 1 — the general transfer pessimism.** In a second-order meta-analysis spanning working-memory training, video games, music, chess, and exergames (14 first-order meta-analyses, 332 samples, ~21,968 participants, k=233 in the largest model), far-transfer effect size **and its true variance both equaled zero** once publication bias and placebo effects were controlled — regardless of population or training type. The authors call failure to generalize "an invariant of human cognition." Near transfer, meanwhile, is robust and replicated. **[T1 — Sala et al. 2019, *Collabra: Psychology* 5(1):18.]** The companion meta-analysis (87 publications, 145 comparisons) found reliable intermediate transfer but no convincing far transfer against treated controls. **[T1 — Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016, *Perspectives on Psychological Science*.]**

**The verifiers flagged a scope limit I am obliged to pass on:** these tested *cognitive-ability training with IQ-like outcomes*, not verbal or social skill domains. Importing their pessimism into speech practice is itself an extrapolation — arguably the same far-transfer reasoning the literature warns against. Label it as the extrapolation it is.

**Leg 2 — the closest published analogue, and it failed.** The 4/3/2 technique (same monologue delivered three times under decreasing time pressure) is the nearest thing in print to your app. The claim that its fluency gains **generalized to a novel topic three days later was voted down 0-3 in adversarial verification.** What did survive: 4/3/2 alone, without corrective feedback, improved speed and pausing but did **not** improve grammatical accuracy (past tense p = .953 and .755; irregular p = .209 and .657), replicating a fluency/accuracy dissociation from Boers 2014 and Thai & Boers 2016. **[T3 — Tran & Saito, *Language Teaching Research*; n ≈ 12 per group; Vietnamese EFL learners drilled on English past-tense morphology.]**

Read that carefully. Monologue-to-monologue transfer *on a new topic in the same format* did not survive verification. Monologue-to-**dialogue** transfer is a much longer jump — different modality, no planning time, real-time comprehension of a partner, turn-taking under a sub-second response deadline. **A timed solo monologue drill most plausibly buys you better timed solo monologues.**

There is a suggestive corroboration from the improv side. The Seppänen group argued in print that the Trier Social Stress Test was the wrong instrument for detecting improv-trained gains precisely *because* a fixed-status, non-responsive jury blocks the reciprocal engagement improv trains — "It takes two to tango just as it takes two to improvise" — and moved their 2021 follow-up to naturally unfolding face-to-face interaction for that reason. **[T3 — Seppänen et al. 2020, *Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology*, DOI 10.1007/s40750-020-00145-1.]** Researchers in this area treat monologue-to-a-non-responsive-audience and real dialogue as **structurally different tasks**. That is an author-stated design judgment, not an experimental finding, but it is a relevant one from people who ran the experiment.

## 2. Diagnosis: which of the five is yours

**The literature does not adjudicate between your five candidate explanations for a non-anxious native adult.** It never framed the question that way. What follows is my construction from your own description plus what the evidence establishes about markers. Treat the reasoning as mine and the citations as real.

### The asymmetry in your own report is the biggest clue, and it argues against your hypothesis

You say ordinary conversation with people you know is fine, and meeting someone new makes you stiff and short-answered. **A capacity deficit does not switch off by audience.** If conceptualization-to-articulation were genuinely broken, it would be broken with your roommate too. What varies with audience familiarity is a *state* variable — attentional allocation, evaluative concern, or how much retrievable shared content you have with that person — not a *capacity*. **[GUESS — this inference is mine. No study in this evidence base tests within-person variance across audience familiarity in non-anxious adults.]**

That does not mean "you're anxious." It means the thing that varies is unlikely to be the thing you think it is.

### A discriminating self-test [GUESS — entirely my construction]

Two weeks, roughly one minute per entry. For each notable speaking episode log: setting; how familiar the audience was; whether you actually had something you knew or cared about to say; self-rated ease 1-5; and a 60-second note answering **"what did I wish I'd said?"**

Then read the pattern:

| If you see | The bottleneck is most likely | Why this discriminates |
|---|---|---|
| Ease flat and low **everywhere**, including alone into a recorder with no audience | Capacity — your hypothesis | Only a genuine capacity deficit is audience-invariant. This is the condition under which the drill app is the right tool. |
| Ease drops specifically with unfamiliar or evaluative audiences, **and the "wished I'd said" note is full** | Self-focus / evaluative monitoring | Full note = you had the content and it did not come out. That is an access-under-load problem, not a formation problem. Corroborating marker: you replay the interaction afterward. |
| The "wished I'd said" note is **thin or empty** | Content | You did not have more to say. Common in stock pitches specifically, where the fix is doing the work on the company, not on the speaking. |
| Ease is flattish but the word or example arrives ~3 seconds late, **consistently, including with friends** | Retrieval speed | Latency without audience-sensitivity. |
| Your short answers follow partner turns whose content you cannot recall | Listening | See the objective version below. |

**The listening branch has a genuinely objective test**, and it is the one I would run first because it is cheap and the underlying construct has real evidence behind it. Record one real conversation (with the other person's consent), transcribe your questions, and classify each using Huang et al.'s taxonomy: *follow-up* (builds on what they just said), *mirror* ("how about you?"), *full-switch* (new topic), *introductory* ("how are you?"). **Follow-up questions specifically were the type that predicted liking; full-switch, mirror, and introductory questions did not predict liking in joint regression.** If your follow-up proportion is low, you have a listening-and-uptake problem wearing a fluency costume — and unlike "be more articulate," it is directly actionable in the next conversation you have.

Benchmark for calibration: under a "ask at least nine questions" manipulation, follow-ups rose from 28.02% to 41.50% of questions asked. **[T2 — Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino 2017, *JPSP* 113(3):430-452.]** Those are the observed levels in a lab dyad; they are not a validated norm and I would not treat 41.5% as a target.

### My honest ranking of the five, for you specifically

1. **Self-focus / evaluative monitoring** — best fits the stated audience-asymmetry. Note this does *not* require clinical anxiety.
2. **Listening / uptake** — fits "short answers" better than a fluency deficit does, and has the only clean objective test.
3. **Content** — fits situation 4 (pitches) specifically, and almost nothing else.
4. **Practice volume** — plausible but untestable from the inside; it is also the explanation most consistent with the near-transfer evidence, since real conversations are the only practice with common elements to real conversations.
5. **Retrieval speed / capacity** — your hypothesis. Ranked last because the audience-asymmetry argues against it, not because the mechanism is fake. The mechanism is real (Felker 2019); the *trait* framing is what does not hold up.

## 3. What actually works, ranked by evidence strength

Including the things you will not like.

### Tier A — real aggregate evidence, but each carries a serious catch

**A1. Asking follow-up questions.** Effects are real and small: Study 1 d = .35; Study 2A β = .28, SE = .09, t(306) = 3.13, p < .001, d = .27. Mediated by perceived responsiveness — standardized effect .12, 95% CI [.01, .25], p = .034 (Study 1) and .07, 95% CI [.00, .14], p = .041 (Study 2A). Follow-ups specifically: β = .14, SE = .02, t(526) = 6.61, p < .001; mediation .06, 95% CI [.01, .11], p = .006. **[T2 — two randomized dyadic experiments plus one observational speed-dating study, Huang et al. 2017 *JPSP*.]**

**The catch, and it is a real one: this line is contested and has not been independently replicated.** Kluger & Malloy (2019) reanalyzed the public speed-dating data and reported a directionally opposite result. The only defense is the original authors' own reply (*JPSP* 117(6):1139-1144), which alleges three core errors and reports a corrected speed-dating correlation of r = .27, p < .01 for second-date offers. **That reply is a rebuttal by the original authors, not independent replication, and no independent replication was located in this run.** Report it as unsettled. The mediation effects (.06–.12) are small even taken at face value.

**The other catch: the manipulation was a single instruction delivered inside a live conversation.** "Ask at least nine questions." No solo app can deliver it. This is the best-evidenced behavior for your top two situations and it is structurally undeliverable by the artifact you built.

**A2. Keeping story events in their real temporal and causal order.** Randomly scrambling event sequence reduces narrative transportation relative to the intact version, across three cited studies. Transportation itself has a large aggregated base: 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles. **[T1 for the construct — van Laer, De Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels 2014, *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5):797-817. T5 for the scrambling finding — Green & Appel 2024 *Advances* preprint p.19, citing Appel et al. 2015, Wang & Calder 2006, Schreiner et al. 2018; those three primaries were not fetched, so direction and study count are supported but effect sizes and Ns are not.]** Caveat worth knowing: scrambling is often deployed as a *scale manipulation check* rather than a purpose-built craft study, which makes it weaker evidence about storytelling technique than it first sounds.

**Everything else you asked about storytelling returned nothing.** Where to start, how much setup listeners tolerate, endings, and — importantly — **whether rehearsing and refining a stock of personal stories improves delivery at all.** That last one is currently craft convention with no located empirical base. Your "story bank" instinct may be right; it is not evidenced.

**A3. Brief third-party observation as a measurement instrument.** Thin slices under five minutes predicted objective outcomes at r = .39 across 38 results. **[T1 — Ambady & Rosenthal 1992, *Psychological Bulletin* 111(2):256-274.]** **Flag: this run verified only what the 1992 meta-analysis reported. Whether thin-slice findings survive modern replication scrutiny — which you explicitly asked about — remains unanswered.** Given that this is 1990s social psychology, treat the r = .39 as the reported figure, not as a number that has been stress-tested the way you would want.

### Tier B — one randomized study each, small, but directly relevant

**B1. Cognitive preparation before any self-review.** Participants randomized to *predict in detail what they would see, form a mental image of themselves giving the speech, then watch as though watching a stranger* rated their performance higher than participants given the same video with no preparation, and higher than their own pre-video ratings — **in both high and low socially anxious participants.** The authors' account: preparation works by maximizing the perceived discrepancy between the distorted self-image and the objective video. **[T2 — Harvey, Clark, Ehlers & Rapee 2000, *Behaviour Research and Therapy* 38:1183-1192. Single session, small, analog high-anxious sample — but the low-anxious half held, which is the closest available read on someone like you.]**

The unprepared self-review arm in that study is *exactly your app's current loop.* It was the comparison condition. It lost.

**B2. Improv, for a specific and limited benefit.** A 7-week / 17.5-hour improv course reduced pre-speech physiological arousal — zygomaticus F(1,30) = 16.79, p < .001; corrugator F(1,32) = 5.12, p = .031; HRV RMSSD p = .037 and STDHR p = .042 — **but only in the 30-second window before speaking, with no cortisol difference and no effects during the speech itself.** **[T3 — Seppänen et al. 2020, n = 19 vs 16 Finnish student teachers.]** A companion study found a significant rise in self-rated interpersonal confidence (N = 19, t(18) = −2.301, p = .034, d = 0.96) with no change in a non-randomized self-selected control (N = 18, t(17) = −0.852, p = .406). **[T3 — Seppänen et al. 2019, *European Journal of Social & Behavioural Sciences* XXIV(1):2770-2788.]**

**Two things about improv you should not skip.** First, the confidence effect was **moderated by starting point**: Johnson-Neyman analysis showed it was significant only below a pre-test score of 2.67 on a 0-5 scale (d = 1.04 in the low subgroup vs d = 0.13 in the high). If you are already socially functional, the evidence predicts you get roughly nothing. Second, **the demonstrated effect is affective, not skill transfer** — the researchers themselves interpret it as reduced *anticipatory* anxiety, not improved speech behavior. Small N, non-randomized control, single research group, unreplicated.

### Tier C — no direct evidence, but the strongest structural bet

**C1. Volume of real conversation.** No study in this evidence base tests it. But near transfer is the robust, replicated finding across every training domain examined, and far transfer is the thing that collapses to zero. Practice that shares its actual elements with the target — a real partner, real turn-taking, no planning time, real stakes — is the only kind the transfer literature says reliably pays. **[Inference from T1 transfer evidence, not a tested claim. Label it as such.]**

**This is the honest headline of the whole report.** If you asked me to bet, I would bet on twenty more real conversations over twenty more recorded monologues, and the transfer literature is why.

### The annoying, inconvenient things — listed because you asked for them

- **Record real conversations, not monologues** (with consent) and count your follow-up question proportion. Socially awkward, logistically irritating, and the only instrument here that measures the thing you actually care about.
- **Ask people for feedback out loud.** Third-party judgment is the measurement channel with meta-analytic support (r = .39); self-report of one's own communication competence has **no verified validity against observer ratings in this evidence base** — that entire question went uncovered.
- **Join the investment fund's discussion, not just its pitch slot.** The pitch is the one situation your app trains. The discussion around it is closer to everything else you want.
- **Take an actual improv class** if and only if your baseline social confidence is genuinely low. The moderation analysis says otherwise it is 17.5 hours for d = 0.13.
- **Stop optimizing the drill and go have the conversation.** Building the training system is more comfortable than the thing it trains. Worth naming.

## 4. Practice protocol

**Read the label before the protocol: the literature does not supply these parameters.** Planning-time dose-response and the 2025 task-repetition meta-analysis both fell out of the verification budget. What follows is a reasonable structure with the evidence attached where it exists and [GUESS] stamped where it does not. I am not going to dress a guess as a finding.

**Daily, ~15 min:**

| Component | Parameter | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Follow-up question practice, in live conversations you were already having | Pick one conversation per day; afterward note your follow-up ratio | Behavior is T2-evidenced (Huang 2017, contested). Frequency and the ratio target are **[GUESS]** |
| Interview/pitch drill (see §5) | 3× per week, 3 reps of the same prompt per session | Repetition improves speed/pausing **[T3, L2, n≈12/group]**. Rep count and weekly frequency **[GUESS]** |
| Cognitive preparation, **mandatory before any playback** | Predict what you'll see → form the image → watch as a stranger | **[T2 — Harvey et al. 2000]**. This is the best-evidenced single parameter in the table |
| Planning window before speaking | Unknown. Your current 30s is as defensible as 10s or 60s | **[UNVERIFIED]** — dose-response literature not verified in this run |
| Total duration before expecting anything | 7 weeks is the only intervention length with any data attached, and that was improv | **[GUESS]** for your case |

**Do not add a filler-word target.** Filled pauses showed no relationship to IQ, inhibition, or set-shifting **[T3 — Engelhardt 2013]**, and whether audiences penalize them as much as speakers assume went **completely unverified** in this run. Meanwhile the claim that pause and speed dominate perceived fluency over self-repairs was **refuted 1-2**. You do not currently have an evidence-backed acoustic target to aim at. Aiming at one anyway is how you end up optimizing a number that does not predict what listeners think.

## 5. What to do with the drill app

### Keep it, but demote it. It is an interview-and-pitch trainer, not a communication trainer. And rebuild the feedback loop.

**Why keep it at all.** The structure — 30s to plan, 60s to speak, no partner — is close to a *literal replica* of a behavioral interview answer and a short pitch open. That is **near transfer**, and near transfer is the robust finding across every domain examined **[T1 — Sala et al. 2019]**. For your situation 4, the app trains the actual task. That is a real and defensible use.

**Why demote it.** For situations 1, 2, and 3 — which you ranked first, second, and third — it is training a different task and asking for far transfer. There is no evidence supporting that jump, and the one directly relevant test we have is negative: 4/3/2's generalization to a merely *novel topic* in the *same format* was **refuted 0-3**. Dialogue adds a partner, turn-taking, comprehension load, and zero planning time. If topic-to-topic transfer does not hold, format-to-format is a much worse bet.

**Say it plainly: for the three situations you care most about, the timed solo monologue app is training the wrong thing.**

**What to rebuild.** The playback-and-self-score loop is the app's **highest-risk component, not its strongest feature.**

- Reviewing observer-perspective third-person images of your own performance **increased next-day post-event rumination** — significant on the negative-Self subscale. **[T2 — Roos et al. 2018, *mHealth*, N = 46, four randomized arms including a no-review control. The authors call it a pilot; effect appeared on one of three subscales; no replication located; high-socially-anxious sample with no non-anxious arm.]**
- Unprepared self-review was the *losing comparison condition* in Harvey et al. 2000. Cognitive preparation is what converts self-review from merely exposing to actually corrective, and it held in low-anxious participants.

So: **either delete the playback loop, or gate it behind a forced cognitive-preparation step.** Predict in detail what you will hear, form the image, then listen as though it is a stranger. Two screens before playback unlocks. That is the single change in this report with the best evidence-to-effort ratio.

Also **kill the single self-score.** Repairs and filled pauses have different causes and one composite score blurs them **[T3 — Engelhardt 2013]**. If you score anything, score the two separately and expect them to move independently.

**What to build instead, if you are going to build.** The highest-value thing an app could do for situations 1 and 2 is not generate prompts — it is **transcribe a real conversation and count your follow-up-question ratio.** That measures the best-evidenced behavior in the entire report, in the setting that actually matters. It is also a harder build and requires consent from the other person, which is precisely why it is the version that would work.

## 6. Instrumentation

**Honest state: the measurement section of your brief is the one that came back emptiest.** PRCA-24, the Personal Report of Public Speaking Anxiety, SIAS/SPS, and whether any self-report communication-competence scale tracks observer ratings — **all uncovered.** Which acoustic metrics predict listener-judged quality — the leading candidate claim (pause and speed dominating over repairs) was **refuted 1-2**, so there is currently **no validated dependent variable** to recommend. Perceived and utterance fluency correlate only r = .20–.76, so the easy-to-compute numbers are not the same thing as the number you care about.

What I would actually track, with the evidence status attached:

| Measure | Cadence | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Follow-up question ratio** in one recorded real conversation | Every 2 weeks | The behavior is T2-evidenced and contested; the taxonomy is real. Best available proxy for the thing that matters |
| **Thin-slice rating** — hand a 3-5 min clip to someone who was not there, ask for a single overall rating | Monthly | Thin-slice validity r = .39 **[T1, 1992, modern replication status unverified]** |
| Repairs per minute and filled pauses per minute, **tracked separately** | Per drill session | They dissociate **[T3]**. Neither has a verified link to listener judgment |
| The audience-asymmetry log from §2 | Daily for 2 weeks, then stop | **[GUESS]** — diagnostic, not a progress metric |

**What real improvement looks like numerically over a semester: I cannot tell you, and neither can this literature.** There is no effect-size-per-week figure to anchor to and no test-retest reliability figure for any of these measures in a native-speaking adult, which means I cannot tell you what movement exceeds noise.

The one defensible thing to say: **effect sizes in this entire area are small.** d = .27–.35 for the question-asking effects, mediation effects of .06–.12. If you are hoping to detect a d = 0.3 change in yourself with n = 1 and no control condition, you cannot. **[GUESS, but a well-founded one]** — treat the follow-up ratio as the only number worth watching, expect it to be noisy, and put more weight on whether the specific conversations feel different than on any tracked figure.

## 7. Open questions and source list

### Open questions, in order of how much they would change the answer

1. **Does any published study test a speaking-specific intervention on an untrained speaking situation?** None found. Without it, the transfer verdict rests on extrapolating cognitive-training pessimism into a domain those meta-analyses never tested. Highest-value gap in the report.
2. **Does unprepared self-review harm non-anxious speakers, or is the rumination risk confined to high-anxiety samples?** The Roos pilot has no non-anxious arm. This single question decides whether your playback loop should be deleted outright or kept behind a cognitive-preparation gate.
3. **Which acoustic or behavioral metrics actually predict listener-judged conversational quality for native speakers?** The leading candidate was refuted. Instrumentation currently has no validated dependent variable.
4. **Is "build a story bank" anything more than craft convention?** Transportation research establishes sequence matters to the *listener*; nothing found on whether a *speaker's* rehearsal improves it.
5. **Has the Huang line been independently replicated since 2019, and do thin-slice findings survive modern replication scrutiny?** Neither was checked in this run.

### Claims that were killed in verification and must not be repeated

- 4/3/2 fluency gains generalize to a novel topic (**0-3**) — the most consequential refutation in the report.
- Pause and speed dominate perceived fluency while self-repairs contribute little (**1-2**).
- First-person image review improves factual recall of the event (**0-3**).
- The "shared resource pool" mechanism reading of Felker et al., and two further restatements of that study, when attributed to the journal landing page rather than the full text (**0-3**). *The raw disfluency effect survived from the full text; the mechanism interpretation did not.*
- The causal framing of the Huang findings when attributed to the HKS summary page (**0-3**). *The causal claim survived from the primary; that particular attribution did not.*
- A restatement of Melby-Lervåg et al. as showing WM training produces only "short-term, task-specific effects that do not generalize" (**0-3**) — an overstatement, since **intermediate transfer was reliable**; it is *far* transfer that failed.
